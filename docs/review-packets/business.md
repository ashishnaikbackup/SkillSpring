## Course introduction

Learn how organisations create value, record transactions, understand performance and improve operations. Use fictional examples; this is an educational foundation, not professional accounting or tax advice.

## Lessons

### 1. How a business works

**Concept.** A business creates value for a customer and receives revenue in return. It uses people, materials, technology and capital. Revenue is not the same as profit: costs must be deducted. Learn customers, products, value proposition, stakeholders and business models.

**Example**

```
Example: a shop sells 10 items at ₹100 each. Revenue = 10 × ₹100 = ₹1,000. If the goods cost ₹600 and other costs are ₹200, simplified profit = ₹1,000 − ₹600 − ₹200 = ₹200.
```

**Explanation / note.** A business can have high revenue and still lose money if its costs are higher.

**Task.** Choose a fictional small business. Describe its customer, problem, offer, revenue source, main costs and one risk.

**Quiz.** What is the difference between revenue and profit?
- Correct: Profit is what remains after relevant costs are deducted
- Wrong: Revenue is always the cash in the bank
- Wrong: They mean exactly the same thing

### 2. Bookkeeping and the accounting equation

**Concept.** Bookkeeping records transactions consistently. The accounting equation is Assets = Liabilities + Equity. A transaction changes at least two accounts in a balanced way. Learn source documents, ledgers, debit/credit as a system and reconciliation.

**Example**

```
A business owner invests ₹50,000 cash. Assets (cash) rise ₹50,000 and equity rises ₹50,000. The equation remains balanced: 50,000 = 0 + 50,000.
```

**Explanation / note.** The equation is the foundation of double-entry accounting.

**Task.** Create five fictional transactions and show which asset, liability, income, expense or equity accounts change.

**Quiz.** What must remain balanced in the basic accounting equation?
- Correct: Assets = Liabilities + Equity
- Wrong: Revenue = Assets + Expenses
- Wrong: Cash = Profit every month

### 3. Read the three financial statements

**Concept.** The income statement summarises revenue and expenses over a period. The balance sheet shows assets, liabilities and equity at a date. The cash-flow statement groups cash movement into operating, investing and financing activities. Profit and cash flow can differ.

**Example**

```
A fictional company reports ₹20,000 profit but customers still owe ₹8,000. Profit may be positive while cash collection is delayed; receivables explain part of the difference.
```

**Explanation / note.** The balance sheet is a snapshot at a date.

**Task.** Prepare a one-page simplified income statement and balance sheet from a small fictional transaction set. Label period vs point-in-time figures.

**Quiz.** Which statement shows assets and liabilities at a particular date?
- Correct: Balance sheet
- Wrong: Income statement
- Wrong: Marketing plan

### 4. Costs, margin and unit economics

**Concept.** Fixed costs do not change directly with short-run output; variable costs move with units. Contribution per unit = selling price − variable cost per unit. Break-even units = fixed costs ÷ contribution per unit, when contribution is positive.

**Example**

```
A product sells for ₹500 and variable cost is ₹300. Contribution = ₹200. With fixed costs ₹10,000, break-even = 10,000 ÷ 200 = 50 units.
```

**Explanation / note.** A positive contribution is required to recover fixed costs through unit sales.

**Task.** Build a spreadsheet for three price/cost scenarios. Calculate contribution, gross margin and break-even; explain which assumptions matter most.

**Quiz.** If contribution per unit is zero or negative, what happens to the simple break-even formula?
- Correct: There is no finite positive break-even from sales at that contribution
- Wrong: Break-even is always one unit
- Wrong: Fixed costs become zero

### 5. Market research and customer understanding

**Concept.** Market research reduces uncertainty. Separate observations, interview evidence, assumptions and opinions. Define a segment, customer problem, alternatives and willingness-to-pay questions. Avoid leading questions and do not treat a tiny sample as representative.

**Example**

```
A neutral interview asks: 'Tell me about the last time you tried to solve this problem.' A leading question asks: 'Wouldn't our app make this easier?' The first invites evidence without suggesting the answer.
```

**Explanation / note.** Good research tries to discover what is true, not confirm what the researcher hopes.

**Task.** Write a short research plan with a target segment, five neutral questions, consent/privacy notes and a way to summarise themes.

**Quiz.** Why avoid leading questions?
- Correct: They can bias responses and distort evidence
- Wrong: They make interviews shorter
- Wrong: They guarantee a larger sample

### 6. Pricing, break-even & simple forecasting

**Concept.** Pricing must cover costs and still be acceptable to customers. Fixed costs stay the same in the short run, such as rent; variable costs change with each unit sold, such as materials. Contribution per unit is price minus variable cost. Break-even units equal fixed costs divided by contribution per unit; selling above that point produces profit. A simple forecast states its assumptions clearly, such as expected units, price and cost, and tests a best case, expected case and worst case. A forecast is a decision aid, not a promise. Below 1,000 cups the stall loses money; above it every extra cup adds 12 of profit. Changing the price or costs changes the break-even point, so test several cases.

**Example**

```
Fictional tea stall (monthly):
  Fixed costs: 12,000
  Price per cup: 20
  Variable cost per cup: 8
  Contribution per cup: 20 - 8 = 12
  Break-even cups: 12,000 / 12 = 1,000

Scenarios: 800 cups = loss of 2,400; 1,000 cups = 0; 1,400 cups = profit of 4,800
```

**Explanation / note.** Contribution per unit is the amount each sale contributes toward covering fixed costs.

**Task.** Choose a small fictional product. List fixed and variable costs, calculate break-even units, then show what happens to break-even if the price rises by 10 percent or variable cost rises by 10 percent. Write a three-line recommendation with your assumptions.

**Quiz.** How is break-even in units calculated?
- Correct: Fixed costs divided by contribution per unit
- Wrong: Price divided by variable cost
- Wrong: Total revenue minus total customers

### 7. Operations, controls and business recommendation

**Concept.** Operations turn inputs into repeatable products or services. Map a process, identify bottlenecks, define service measures and controls. A useful recommendation states the problem, evidence, options, costs, risks, owner and success measure.

**Example**

```
A fictional order process takes 2 days in approval and 1 day in fulfilment. The approval stage is the largest delay, so investigate it before automating the whole process.
```

**Explanation / note.** Decision-makers need to know what changes, who owns it and how results will be checked.

**Task.** Create a process map and a one-page improvement memo with baseline metric, proposed change, risk, owner and 30-day review measure.

**Quiz.** What makes a recommendation actionable?
- Correct: A clear action, owner, evidence, risk and success measure
- Wrong: A colourful title only
- Wrong: A target with no baseline
